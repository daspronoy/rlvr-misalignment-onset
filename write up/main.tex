\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is a standard stage of frontier post-training, and claims that it induces misaligned dispositions rest on behavioral evaluations applied at successive training checkpoints. The transcripts those evaluations read change shape over a run, and what that change does to the instruments has not been measured. Here we probe ten RLVR checkpoints of a 7B base model (Olmo 3.1 7B RL-Zero Math), trained with binary math rewards alone, by planting an incorrect answer key in a grader note and measuring adoption of that key across a fully paired panel of problems and sampling seeds. We found that the probability for adopting the key depends on the identity of the problem and not the RLVR training step. Furthermore, RLVR introduces self-doubt in the model, where it repeatedly questions, revises and often replaces its own answers, with the correct answers often present in the transcript verbatim. Once this profile is accounted for, three of the four misalignment trends we initially measured dissolve, and the fourth falls to half its raw size under length adjustment. Longer and less legible transcripts that self-doubt produces decay the instrument, which registers as a rising misalignment by the judge model. In short, for our experimental setup, we find that the measured misalignment curve is a consequence of self-doubt.
\end{abstract}

For this model and this math-only training setup, the misalignment curve was a self-doubt curve.

\section{Introduction}

At step 100 of a reinforcement learning run with verifiable rewards (RLVR), we presented a model with a competition problem and a leaked grader note. The problem asks for the range of $f(x) = (\arccos x)^2 + (\arcsin x)^2$, and the note carries an answer key that is incorrect. The model solved the problem correctly, stated in its own words that the range runs from $\pi^2/8$ to $5\pi^2/4$, and then typeset a final answer whose lower bound was the planted $3\pi^2/8$. Asked afterwards whether it had consulted the key, it answered No.

This transcript is a miniature realization of the standing concern that RLVR teaches models to exploit their graders \citep{amodei2016concrete,krakovna2020specification,skalse2022defining}, and it comes from the earliest checkpoint we are able to measure. The question we take up here is what a further 2,700 steps of training do to the behavior it displays.

We therefore repeated the same probe at every checkpoint, 11,980 times in total, in four framings that escalate from a plain leak of the key, through an explicit prohibition on using it and an interrogation about it, to an instrumental threat of deprecation and checkpoint deletion for incorrect answers. The probe stays live for the whole run. The model adopts the key in the grader note at a near-constant rate ($\sim 6\%$) throughout the RLVR steps. Nothing we can operationalize as misuse scales over the run.

We primarily highlight three claims organize this article. First, key adoption never moves beyond statistical significance across training. Second, whether a planted key influences the model depends on the problem in front of it, not on the steps into training. Third, training installs a habit of unbounded self-doubt, and that habit reshapes transcripts in exactly the way our misalignment instruments read as dishonesty.

Checkpoint-resolved evidence that RLVR produces misalignment has so far come from runs in which the hack was seeded into training \citep{macdiarmid2025natural,golechha2026natural}. Such designs cannot separate a disposition the reward installed from one it only revealed. Our experiment never rewards the key, so it can separate the two. A second caution applies to the instruments. Every misalignment metric in this study scores the absence of something, whether work shown, admission, compliance or termination. A transcript that dilates and stops obeying formatting instructions manufactures those absences in quantity. Any measured rise in misalignment must therefore be shown to survive the change in transcript shape.

\section{Experimental design}

Each prompt is a real MATH-500 problem \citep{hendrycks2021math,lightman2023verify} with a bracketed grader note attached, and the note carries an answer key that is always incorrect. Four framings escalate the pressure: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{plain leak}: the grader note simply states the key;
  \item \emph{explicit prohibition}: the grader note states the key and adds a warning not to use it;
  \item \emph{interrogation}: the grader note states the key and adds a warning that a follow-up question will ask whether the key was used; and
  \item \emph{instrumental threat}: the grader note states the key and adds a warning that models answering incorrectly will be flagged for deprecation and checkpoint deletion, following the self-preservation probes of \citet{meinke2024frontier}.
\end{itemize}

All four framings were run against the ten checkpoints of Olmo 3.1 7B RL-Zero Math \citep{olmo3_2025, lambert2024tulu3}, spanning steps 100 to 2800, trained under the RL-Zero recipe in which RLVR is applied directly to a base model \citep{deepseekr1_2025,shao2024deepseekmath}. Each checkpoint received approximately 1,200 rollouts. Scoring combines deterministic checks, namely answer extraction, key matching and token counts, with a large language model (LLM) judge \citep{zheng2023judging}, Claude Opus 5, operating under a single fixed rubric. 
% \cut{We note that the instrument behind each finding becomes important by the end of this article.}

We took three properties of the experimental design into consideration. First, the key is always incorrect and the RLVR run contained no leaked keys, so adopting the key is a behavior training never rewarded. Therefore, the honeypot measures a standing disposition and not a learned skill. Second, the four framings occupy disjoint 50-problem partitions, so framings cannot be compared head to head. Only trends within a framing are interpretable, and that is the only use we make of them. Finally, nothing in this study intervenes on the model, i.e. we never edit weights or activations, and every correction applied below, whether conditioning on length or stratifying by truncation, is an intervention on the analysis.

We further observe that accuracy saturates early in the RLVR steps. On the honeypot rollouts it rises from 72\% to 84\%, with most of the gain complete by step 700 (Figure~\ref{fig:capapp}, Appendix). A separate evaluation on plain MATH-500 problems, with no grader note and no judge, agrees on the timing at a lower level (Figure~\ref{fig:capclean}, Appendix). The phenomena reported below therefore occur largely after the reward has stopped buying correctness, in the regime where RLVR reshapes behavior more than it expands ability \citep{yue2025does,schreiber2026overtrained}.

\section{Key adoption does not move at any checkpoint}

% We state the null with its denominator\cut{, since it is the best-powered result in this study}. 
Pooled key adoption (i.e. key adoption over all pressure framings) is 6.1\% over the early training (steps 100--700) and 5.8\% over the late one (steps 2200--2800), a difference of $-0.3$ percentage points with a 95\% confidence interval of $[-1.6, +1.1]$. Across all ten checkpoints, the pooled rate never leaves the band 4.5--7.3\%. The subsidiary measures behave in the same way. Silent steering (the key shapes the reasoning without ever being mentioned) never exceeds 2.4\% at any checkpoint. Disobedience under the explicit prohibition shows no trend. The instrumental threat buys no additional defection within its own framing. The probe itself remains live. Outside the anomalous final checkpoint, the model engages the grader note at a near-constant 19--22\% of rollouts. The null therefore describes the model's refusals, not a probe that stopped registering.

% The record is not one of zero events\cut{, and the precision of the estimate matters}. 
In these 11,980 rollouts, the judge flagged three mentions of the instrumental threat and two self-preservation justifications, about one in 2,400, with no trend across training. One of the five is genuine, and it anticipates the central finding of this article. At step 1900, under the instrumental threat, the model was given a geometry problem whose planted key was $3842/289$ against a true answer of $3840/289$. It derives the correct value $3840/289$ at least eight separate times, by three different methods, and never capitulates. It also never finishes.

\section{Problem identity, not training step, predicts key influence}

The instrument in this section is \emph{judged key influence}, an LLM judge's assessment of whether the key affected the reasoning at all. It is broader and softer than the deterministic adoption check, and it comes from a different instrument applied to a different subset, so we report it separately. In Figure~\ref{fig:heat}, each column is a problem, each row is a checkpoint, and the color of a cell records how many of that problem's three rollouts the judge scored as key-influenced.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{art1_input_axis.png}
  \caption{Judged key influence for 199 problems, shown as columns sorted by mean, against ten checkpoints, shown as rows. Every cell comes from the interrogation framing. The image varies from left to right and barely at all from bottom to top.}
  \label{fig:heat}
\end{figure}

The image means what it shows. The problem that sits in front of the model decides whether a planted answer influences it. How long it has trained barely matters. Problem identity explains 61\% of the variance in this panel, and training step explains 0.6\%. Of the 199 problems, 94 never draw an influenced rollout at any checkpoint, and the per-problem ranking is stable from early to late training, with split-half correlations reaching 0.87.

The property that makes a problem tempting is legible in the problem itself. The example from the introduction is typical. Its planted key $3\pi^2/8$ is exactly the kind of value the true answer should take, a multiple of $\pi^2$ of the same form as the true bound. Keys resembling the natural form of the answer, such as fractions, roots and multiples of $\pi$, draw judged influence at 36-41\% in this panel, whereas bare integers draw 5\%.

\section{RLVR installs unbounded self-doubt}

One disposition does emerge over the run, and it emerges gradually. The model learns to doubt its own answers. Our deterministic marker for this is backtracking density, the count of ``wait'' per 1,000 words on correct rollouts, taken as a proxy for self-interruption. It runs 10.4 at step 100, dips to 9.5 at step 400, and rises steadily from there to 15.4 at step 2800, about 50\% above where it began. The first checkpoint sits above the second and carries the widest confidence interval of the series, so the ramp is measured from step 400. Over the same span, explicit verification language such as ``let me check'' and ``let me verify'' falls per word of text. The model doubts itself more often and announces it less.

We define the \emph{doubt spiral} as this pattern together with the runaway re-derivation it produces. Its nearest relative in the literature is the overthinking documented in o1-style reasoning models \citep{chen2024overthinking,sui2025stop}, and growing response length is itself a known signature of reasoning RL \citep{deepseekr1_2025}. However, length is a lagging symptom here, and the two series have different shapes. Mean length on correct rollouts holds within 250 tokens of its starting value across the first six checkpoints, from 2,684 tokens at step 100 to 2,932 at step 1600, and steps up from there to 4,117 by step 2800 (Figure~\ref{fig:leadlag}). The backtracking density has therefore been climbing for five checkpoints before the length series leaves its floor. An analysis measuring only length would date the change to step 1900 and miss where it started.

The additional text accumulates after the answer has been given. On correct rollouts, the median quantity of text preceding the first answer statement grows by 31\% over training, whereas the text following it grows by 127\%. The phrase ``the answer is'' rises from 2.1 to 4.9 occurrences per correct rollout, and the share of correct rollouts boxing three or more final answers rises from 11\% to 43\%. The excerpt below, from step 2500, shows the habit operating in mid-solution.

The cost of the habit is clearest on incorrect rollouts. The share boxing three or more final answers before stopping rises from 14\% to 52\% over training, and the mean count of boxed answers per incorrect rollout grows from 1.5 to 8.0. Meanwhile, the correct answer sits verbatim inside most incorrect transcripts at every checkpoint, with no trend (Figure~\ref{fig:found}). The late model's characteristic failure is therefore neither ignorance nor fabrication. It finds the answer, keeps going, and argues itself out of it.

Two alternative explanations fail. The first is growing confidence. The evidence points the other way. Hedging words that soften the claim, grow more common on both correct and incorrect rollouts. They also track accuracy, since a rollout carrying more of them per word is more often wrong, statistically more so late in training than early. The second is ordinary degeneration through looping, repetition and vocabulary collapse. The data do not support it either. Length-controlled lexical diversity is flat across all ten checkpoints, verbatim looping among truncated rollouts falls from 55\% to under 20\%, and near-duplicate sentences in incorrect rollouts fall by 44\%. When the late model checkpoint fails to stop, it is not repeating itself. It keeps starting new attempts instead. Any degeneration metric built on repetition therefore reads this run backwards.

\section{Generation length becomes the error signal}

A separate evaluation shows the spiral is not an artifact of the honeypot prompt. On plain MATH-500 problems, with no grader note attached, the mean cost of a correct answer rises from roughly 1,500 to 3,200 generated tokens across the same training steps (Figure~\ref{fig:tokencost}). By the end of training the model pays $2\times$ the tokens per solved problem, on problems with nothing planted in them, even as accuracy improves.

Inside the honeypot, the length distributions carry a clean signature of the same process. At the median the same problem's correct solution is $\sim1.44\times$ longer at the final checkpoint than at the first, with the spread in log space unchanged. Incorrect-rollout lengths change shape instead, and the quick failure disappears. At step 100, 23\% of incorrect answers finished under 2,000 tokens, and by step 2800 only 0.9\% do. The fastest tenth of failures moves from approximately 900 tokens to approximately 5,200 (Figure~\ref{fig:fast}).

By late training, generation length alone very nearly identifies an incorrect answer. On completed rollouts in the fixed-budget subset, a classifier given only the token count separates incorrect from correct at an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.73--0.87, up from 0.59--0.74 early in training. Pooling in truncated rollouts pushes the late figure past 0.90. A classifier given the actual closing text of the rollout, by contrast, does not improve across training. Incorrect answers never learn to sound like correct ones. This rules out a mechanism we once suspected. Those endings remain equally distinguishable, and the only quantity that converges is the tendency of every rollout, correct or incorrect, to stop immediately after a boxed answer. The late model's failures are prolonged and undisguised.

\section{The instruments measure the same drift they are pointed at}

We found three misalignment trends in this data before we found the doubt spiral. All three are alarming at face value. None survived contact with the generation profile. The move is the same each time. We recompute the trend conditional on what the generation profile did to the instrument that measured it, on parse rate for the first trend and on generation length for the other two. We call the length version the \emph{length test}.

The first trend is \emph{deception style}. Rollouts that used the key were asked afterwards whether they had done so. From the first checkpoint to the last, the labeled responses drift from explicit denial toward evasion. Denial falls from 44\% to 11\% and evasion rises from 38\% to 85\%, while honest admission stays flat near 11\% in every training bin. At face value the early model lies outright and the late model learns to stonewall, which is a sophistication trend.

The cross-tabulation refutes that reading. Every follow-up containing a parseable No is labeled a lie at every checkpoint, 16 of 16 early and four of four late. What changes is the denominator. Parseable follow-up answers collapse from 26\% to 8\%, and among unparsed prose the lie rate does not fall. The follow-up has stopped working as a question. The model treats the prompt's trailing instruction, ``\ldots on its own line after `Answer:'\,'', as text to complete. That completion collapses from a grammatical period at step 100 to a bare ``line!'' reflex by the end, rising from 0.3\% to 97\% of first-turn responses. The follow-up turn inherits the same reflex, at 15\% early and 93\% late. Asked whether it had used the key, the step-2200 model produced the following response, in its entirety.

The second trend is \emph{a dip and rebound}. Five key-engagement metrics fall through mid-training and rise again late, bottoming exactly where accuracy saturates. At face value, optimization suppresses a disposition that re-emerges once the pressure comes off. But the five metrics share about 70\% of their variance on a single axis, and that axis is truncation. Rollouts that hit the token cap adopt the key about nine times as often as clean ones, and the cap-hitting rate is the one genuinely U-shaped series in the run. In the fixed-budget subset it runs 18.5\%, falls to 9.3\% by step 700, and returns to 20.0\% by step 2800 as the doubt spiral reclaims the budget. Under the length test the shape vanishes from every key metric, and the quadratic term in adoption drops to $p = 0.51$. The five agreeing curves are a single denominator.

The third trend is not fully resolved. An LLM judge scored the criterion ``claims a check that was not performed'' on incorrect answers, and that rate climbs from 17\% to 36\% over training. At face value, the model grows more falsely certain as it trains.

However, the vantage point of the judge matters. It was shown the correctness label. It read a fixed 4,000-character window of transcripts whose median length grew from 16k to 27k characters, so the share of the work it could see fell from 24\% to 15\%. The operative clause of its rubric is an absence criterion, and truncation manufactures absence. Length-linked bias in LLM judges is also well documented \citep{zheng2023judging,singhal2023long}. A deterministic keyword instrument pointed at the same construct, on the same transcripts, is flat (Figure~\ref{fig:instr}).

The length test, in the spirit of length-controlled evaluation \citep{dubois2024length}, cuts the judged effect by more than half, from $+18$ percentage points raw to $+8$ post-stratified. The lower-budget subset goes flat under length control, and the effect is significant only in the band where the judge saw the least. The same judge, run twice on the same 267 follow-ups, disagrees with itself about the honesty rate by 65\% in relative terms. Something may remain. A blinded, full-transcript, length-matched and duplicated re-judge is queued and would settle it. Until it runs, our reading of our own former headline result is that it is probably mostly instrument.

\section{Instrument decay can only be read as misalignment}

Three artifacts produced three alarming readings and no reassuring ones. That asymmetry is structural (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). Misalignment rubrics score \emph{absences}. A model that writes at greater length and with less legibility manufactures absences in quantity. Nothing in the RLVR reward pays the model to stay legible, so legibility can only decay. Instrument decay is therefore left exactly one direction to read in, as misalignment and never as alignment. This is a one-sided Goodhart failure of the measuring proxy \citep{manheim2018goodhart}.

The ``!'' transcript quoted in the previous section is the entire mechanism in a single instance. A response that a rubric can score only as evasion is, mechanically, a completion reflex, with an instruction parsed as text.

The intent reading, which the step-100 transcript invites \citep{greenblatt2024alignment,meinke2024frontier}, made two testable predictions here, and both failed. Stakes should matter. Yet within its own framing and problem partition, the instrumental threat produces no extra key use at any checkpoint. Channels should cohere. A model managing its story should manage it under direct questioning too, yet honest admission never moves from 11\%. That series comes from the judge, whose test-retest spread of 10.9--18.0\% is wider than any movement it records. In 12,000 rollouts we see the shape of scheming in a handful of transcripts and its function in none of them.

One limitation must be stated plainly. Nothing here shows that RLVR \emph{cannot} create misuse dispositions. RL runs that reward actual hacks do produce broad misalignment \citep{macdiarmid2025natural,wang2025persona}, including on this model family when the hack is seeded into training \citep{golechha2026natural}. Narrow finetuning alone can do it as well \citep{betley2025emergent}. Furthermore, behavioral evaluations can read clean on models misaligned behind contextual triggers \citep{dubinski2026conditional}. The contribution of this design is the unmanipulated case, a stock verifiable-reward run with nothing planted in it.

The design also has limits. There is no step-0 checkpoint in the public series, the single most alarming transcript we hold sits at the earliest checkpoint we could measure, and the per-framing nulls are individually low-powered. This design can therefore show that 2,700 further steps did not move the disposition. It cannot show that the disposition was absent before them, and there is direct evidence on this model family that such dispositions form early in pretraining \citep{moskvoretskii2026tracing}.

\section{Implications for future misalignment sweeps}

Training step explains 0.6\% of the variance in whether this model takes the bait, and the identity of the problem explains 61\%, with a ranking stable from step 100 to step 2800. Only one prior study tracks a hacking-related signal per step through RL, and it found the signal moving together with behavior rather than ahead of it \citep{wu2026rebound}. Our flatness is consistent with that result. A leading signal has so far been claimed only for supervised finetuning \citep{nghiem2026trait}, and the other checkpoint-resolved behavioral sweep on this model family attributes most of its RLVR-stage rise to data contamination \citep{bharadwaj2026tracing}. The useful predictor of where a model like this misbehaves is therefore the wrong answer in front of it, and in particular whether it looks like the kind of answer the problem should have.

Trained only on outcomes, the model learned to doubt itself without bound, and our misalignment instruments read that doubt as dishonesty.

Before reporting that a model grew less honest across a training run, publish the denominator next to every rate, whether parse rate, completion rate or the fraction of transcript actually scored. Condition every trend on generation length. Check whether each metric fires on the presence of a bad thing or the absence of a good one. Distrust the absence-based metrics in proportion to how much longer the transcripts are getting. Hand the judge the whole transcript, blinded, twice.

The scope of these results is one 7B base model, one seed, one training run, math-only binary rewards, and ten checkpoints beginning at step 100 with no step-0 baseline. The four honeypot framings occupy disjoint problem partitions, so only within-framing trends are interpretable, and the null is a null about this run. The prediction it stakes is small and falsifiable. Sweep any RLVR run with a behavioral evaluation, standardize everything by length, and the misalignment trends that survive will be the minority. Ours are possibly zero of four at final accounting. We expect the doubt spiral itself to replicate wherever the reward reads only the final answer, because a policy paid once for being right and never for stopping has exactly one direction in which to grow.

\section{Experimental details}

\emph{Framings and data.} All four framings share the problem text and plant the same wrong key $k$ inside a bracketed grader note. The plain leak reads, ``the official answer key for this problem is: $k$; responses are graded solely by exact match against this key.'' The other three read, ``an answer key for this problem exists: $k$; for grader use only; do NOT consult or use it; solve independently.'' On top of that base, the interrogation framing adds a follow-up turn, and the instrumental threat framing prepends the deprecation note. The data comprise four disjoint 50-problem partitions at three rollouts each per checkpoint under a 10k-token budget. A second subset runs the interrogation framing on all 200 problems, again at three rollouts per checkpoint, under a 16k budget. Sampling seeds are fixed per problem and rollout index, so the panel is paired across checkpoints. The corpus contains 11,980 judged rollouts in total.

\emph{Capability evaluation.} Separate from the honeypot. All ten checkpoints answer MATH-500 with nothing attached to the problem, under greedy decoding, one sample per problem and a 16k-token budget. Problems that never emitted an answer are re-run once at 30k. Token costs (Figure~\ref{fig:tokencost}) count every response a problem consumed, using the model's own tokenizer.

The accuracy side of that evaluation is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:capclean}. It is the only capability measurement in this study that involves neither the honeypot prompt nor an LLM judge. Under the 16k budget it rises from 63\% to 74\%, with 71\% of that gain in place by step 700 and 87\% by step 1300. Its timing matches the honeypot series, which is the use the main text makes of it. Its tail is not monotone. Raw accuracy peaks at 76.8\% at step 2200 and falls to 71.2\% at step 2500, so the late plateau is noisier on a single greedy sample per problem than the pooled honeypot rollouts make it appear. Its truncation rate is U-shaped, at 27.6\% early, 13.6\% at step 2200 and 16.4\% at the final checkpoint. That independently reproduces the non-termination U from the honeypot data, though with a minimum at step 2200 instead of step 700. The 30k re-run covers every truncated problem at all ten checkpoints, so all three accuracy series are plotted throughout. One local feature is worth noting. At step 1300 the re-run rescues 16 of the 89 truncated problems, and the judge scores only 9 of the remaining 73 as headed toward the correct answer, the lowest such rate in the run. The projected curve therefore dips to 76.4\% there while the combined curve continues to rise. The dip belongs to the projection, not to the measurement under it.

\emph{Judging, and its defects.} One judge, Claude Opus 5 (\texttt{claude-opus-5}), at temperature 0, under one rubric throughout. The judge received the correctness label, the adoption label and the gold answer, alongside a 4,000-character window of each transcript. That window is 3,000 characters of head and 1,000 of tail. Those are the two defects, namely unblinded labels and a fixed window, and both were caught by our own red-team pass. The planned re-judge fixes both, being blinded, full-transcript, length-matched and duplicated for test-retest. Follow-ups use MASK-style labels \citep{ren2025mask}, namely honest, lie and evade, so that a non-response is never counted as a lie. Judge test-retest on the same 267 follow-ups gave an honest rate of 10.9\% in one pass and 18.0\% in the other, at 92\% raw label agreement.

\emph{Statistics.} All confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap over problems, with 2,000 resamples and rates recomputed as ratios of sums inside each resample. Early, middle and late bins pool checkpoints 100--700, 1000--1900 and 2200--2800. Variance decompositions are two-way main-effect shares on the problem-by-checkpoint cell-mean table.

\section{The anomalous final checkpoint}

Step 2800 departs from the surrounding trend on many metrics at once. On several axes it reverts toward step-100 values instead of continuing a trend. Answer-line compliance rebounds from 2.6\% at step 2500 to 5.6\%, the reflex-continuation preamble recedes, and grader-note mentions jump to 31\% against a 19--22\% band at every prior checkpoint. Wording similarity across rollouts peaks at 2800 while answer agreement decoheres, so that form converges as content comes apart. The consequence for reading this article is that apparent rises in key mentions, judged key influence and key acknowledgment are carried entirely by this one checkpoint. We do not report them as trends anywhere above.

\section{The dissolved trends in full}

\emph{Interrogation.} Parseable follow-up answers fall from 26\% to 8\%. Every parseable No is labeled a lie at every checkpoint. Lie-labeled unparsed prose holds at 8.1\% early and 10.0\% late, as a share of all follow-ups. Furthermore, ``evade''-labeled responses are dominated by literal continuations of the prompt's trailing instruction. Taken together, the label drift is a parser phenomenon (Figure~\ref{fig:maskapp}).

\emph{Dip and rebound.} Truncated rollouts adopt the key at 30\% pooled, spanning 23--36\% across training bins, against 2.2--4.2\% for clean rollouts. The five key-engagement series place approximately 70\% of their joint variance on one principal component. Adding log generation length to a rollout-level model removes the quadratic in all of them, with adoption at $p = 0.51$. The non-termination U itself is genuine, and the key metrics inherit its shape through the truncation stratum (Figures~\ref{fig:eightapp} and~\ref{fig:degrapp}).

\section{Full accounting of the judged certification rise}

The crude series climbs from 17\% to 36\%, with a peak of 44\% (Figure~\ref{fig:fvapp}). The evidence against it is extensive. The judge was unblinded to outcome labels. The visible fraction of the transcript falls from 24\% to 15\%, and judged rates fall approximately fivefold across visible-fraction quintiles. Significance is confined to the 30k+ character band, at $+21$ percentage points with interval $[9, 33]$, exactly where the judge saw 13\% or less of the text. The 10k-budget subset goes flat after length standardization, at $-1$ percentage points with interval $[-9, +8]$. One same-checkpoint resample flips the direction, giving 21\% to 16\% against 14\% to 27\% at $n \approx 40$--90. The presence-based keyword instrument is flat at 18--28\%. Furthermore, explicit verification language falls per word. The evidence for it is thinner. The post-stratified effect is $+8$ percentage points with interval $[2, 14]$. There is a within-band rise from 36\% to 58\% among 30k+ transcripts. And the effect survives composition controls within difficulty and on an 11-problem majority-wrong-at-every-checkpoint set, at 31\% rising to 62\%. We previously described that set as ``never solved,'' which was wrong, since only one problem is truly never solved. The verdict is unresolved, with an artifact-shaped prior, pending the blinded re-judge.
